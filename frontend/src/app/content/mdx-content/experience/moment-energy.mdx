## Core Contributions

### Telemetry Dashboard Query Optimization

**Impact:** Live telemetry API response times fell by approximately **70%**. Supported query ranges expanded from days to weeks, and long-running queries went from minutes to seconds.

The battery management system (BMS) continuously generated battery pack measurements, but the legacy telemetry backend made dashboard queries slow. I worked on refactoring the service and helped diagnose, migrate, and redesign the aggregation pipeline from Athena through the backend to the dashboard.

**My implementation work included:**

- Writing Athena aggregation queries and moving expensive calculations from application code into SQL to reduce backend processing.
- Implementing timestamp-based downsampling with aggregation levels based on the selected time range, controlling the amount of data returned for longer queries.
- Reducing redundant frontend processing so the dashboard received data is already prepared for visualization.

For very long query ranges, I also implemented a two-scan strategy that I used occasionally. One scan returned regularly downsampled data, while the other used standard deviation and z-scores to identify notable readings. Combining the results helped retain points that regular downsampling could miss.

Reducing the number of points raised another engineering question: how could we keep brief anomalies visible when users queried longer time ranges? That led to my work on live anomaly detection using an exponential moving average (EMA).

### Preserving Anomalies During Downsampling

**The problem:** A smaller chart payload can hide an important event. For example, readings selected five minutes apart might barely differ even though a sharp spike occurred between them. Depending on the aggregation method, that spike could disappear or be diluted in the displayed data.

I used the live MQTT telemetry stream to track a running EMA and identify anomalous timestamps to keep during downsampling. Detecting the event while processing incoming readings gave us information that could be lost once the data had been reduced for visualization.

We stored the timestamps identified by the live anomaly detector. When a long-range query covered those timestamps, we included their readings alongside the downsampled data regardless of whether regular downsampling would select them. This kept detected spikes visible while reducing the overall chart payload.

The choice of baseline mattered because it determined which timestamps to retain. It needed to represent a sensor's recent behavior as its normal operating level changed.

#### Alternatives I explored: Welford and Chan

I explored Welford's running mean and variance for a running z-score, along with Chan's parallel variance formula. Both offered compact statistical summaries without retaining the full time-series history in detector memory.

Welford updates a count `n`, mean `mean`, and sum of squared deviations `M2` for each new reading `x`:

```text
n_new    = n + 1
delta    = x - mean
mean_new = mean + delta / n_new
M2_new   = M2 + delta * (x - mean_new)

sample_variance = M2_new / (n_new - 1)
```

Sample variance requires at least two readings. A new reading can be scored against the prior summary using `z = (x - mean) / sqrt(sample_variance)`, with sufficient history and nonzero variance.

Chan's formula merges two summaries, A and B:

```text
n     = n_A + n_B
delta = mean_B - mean_A
mean  = mean_A + delta * n_B / n
M2    = M2_A + M2_B + delta^2 * n_A * n_B / n
```

This lets you combine batches or partitions without replaying their readings. Chan's formula merges statistical summaries; it is not an anomaly rule by itself. The recurrence and merge approaches are discussed by [Chan, Golub, and LeVeque](https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/litj/notes/numer_anal/AmerStat_37_242_Chan_Variance.pdf).

For my use case, the limitation of a cumulative baseline was how it treated history. When every reading since startup contributes equally, older operating conditions continue to influence what counts as normal. As the count grows, each new reading has less effect on the cumulative mean. Chan's merge formula preserves those cumulative statistics; it does not introduce a preference for recent data.

That does not mean Welford inherently loses the ability to detect spikes. The concern was how well a baseline covering the entire history represented a sensor whose normal level could change.

#### Why I chose EMA

EMA is also commonly used in financial charts to follow price trends. That familiar application illustrates the same idea I used for telemetry: a running baseline that gives recent readings more weight.

EMA gave recent readings more influence while keeping only a compact running baseline per sensor:

```text
ema_t = alpha * x_t + (1 - alpha) * ema_previous
```

Here, `x_t` is the incoming reading and `alpha` is a smoothing factor between 0 and 1. A larger `alpha` adapts faster; a smaller one produces a steadier baseline. Older readings receive exponentially decreasing weight, as described in [NIST's explanation of exponential smoothing](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section4/pmc43.htm).

The distinction is between the baseline and the anomaly decision. EMA alone does not flag a spike: the detector evaluates how far a reading departs from the baseline. Conceptually, that departure can be expressed as:

```text
deviation_t = abs(x_t - ema_previous)
```

This illustrates comparison with the baseline before incorporating the new reading; it is not a reconstruction of the production threshold rule. The important design choice was to use recent behavior to identify timestamps worth retaining, rather than relying only on the points selected for a chart.

EMA was a good fit for that goal because it adapted as a sensor's operating level changed without storing its full history. The tradeoff was responsiveness: a baseline that adapts too quickly can follow an anomaly, while one that adapts too slowly can lag behind a legitimate change in operation.

### Cloud Fault Detection System

**Impact:** The fault detection feature was deployed to production, automatically generating fault events from live telemetry and surfacing them through dashboard alerts and AWS SES email notifications.

I contributed to the system's implementation across telemetry processing, fault retrieval, and notifications. The system consumed live measurements from AWS IoT Core MQTT topics and evaluated them against configurable thresholds retrieved from backend services.

**My implementation work included:**

- Building NestJS monitoring services to consume MQTT telemetry and evaluate threshold-based fault conditions.
- Caching threshold configurations to reduce repeated database lookups during telemetry processing.
- Implementing Athena-backed fault retrieval APIs and supporting historical investigation through Parquet-based data warehousing.
- Integrating fault detection with frontend dashboard alerts and email notifications through AWS SES.

#### Sensor Hysteresis and Fault Recovery

I also worked on hysteresis for transitions between fault severity levels. A sensor reading could move from normal to a high warning and then to a more severe high fault. Recovery needed to account for the current state: clearing the more severe fault did not necessarily mean the reading had returned to normal.

Separate trigger and recovery thresholds controlled these transitions. For example, a reading could trigger the more severe high fault above its upper threshold, then clear that state only after falling below its recovery threshold. If the high-warning condition still applied, the sensor would step back down to a warning rather than immediately returning to normal.

The gap between activation and recovery thresholds helped prevent readings near a boundary from repeatedly switching fault states. This made recovery behavior explicit while reducing state changes caused by small sensor fluctuations.

Together, these components connected live fault detection with notifications and historical fault retrieval, giving users a way to investigate detected conditions after an alert.

## Reflection

This work taught me to evaluate performance and information loss together. Moving calculations into SQL and reducing chart payloads made telemetry faster to explore, but a useful dashboard also needed to retain evidence of short-lived anomalies. Comparing cumulative statistics with EMA helped me connect an algorithm's treatment of history to a product requirement: showing the moments that matter, even across longer time ranges.
